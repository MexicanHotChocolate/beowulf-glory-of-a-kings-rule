    An interlaced exhibit on Beowulf

    The Glory of a King's Rule

    How does Beowulf's role as a leader change throughout the text?

    How does Beowulf's maturity develop him as a leader to his people?

    Start →

    Our thesis

    Beowulf matures as a leader as he learns to use his strength effectively, apply his wisdom, and make sacrifices for the good of his people, becoming a better leader.

    Follow both strands at the same time.

    The poem jumps between different times instead of telling the story in order, like the knotwork on Anglo-Saxon gold where strands go over and under each other. John Leyerle calls this interlace. We pulled out two strands:

    • Gold is Beowulf as a warrior: courage, strength, glory.

    • Blue is Beowulf as a leader: wisdom, protection, sacrifice.

    How the passages connect.

    The line is the whole poem, from line 1 to line 3182, with the six passages where they really are. Each arch connects two of them. Click a word to read the connection.

    1. 01. Fighting Grendel without weapons. Strength, used to win glory.
    2. 02. Beowulf's confidence and desire for glory. Pride in his own strength.
    3. 03. Strong in body, mature in mind. Here the leader strand begins.
    4. 04. Beowulf as an older peaceful king. Fifty years of peace.
    5. 05. His responsibility as king before fighting the dragon. The leader and the warrior in one sentence.
    6. 06. Fifty years, in his own words. Here the gold strand snaps.

    1. Same courage, a new reason.

      Young Beowulf gives up his weapons to make Hygelac famous. Old Beowulf will not line up a huge army against the dragon. His courage and self-pride stay the same, but his leadership shifts from personal fame to defending his people.

    2. Glory, then duty.

      At Heorot he boasts that he is as dangerous as Grendel, to prove his strength and glory. Before the dragon he speaks “as king of the people.” He fights the dragon because he feels it is his duty to protect them.

    3. Hrothgar saw it coming.

      Hrothgar tells the young Beowulf he is “strong in body and mature in mind.” Those thanks forebode his next fifty years as a peaceful, strong ruler whose wisdom only grows.

    4. Fifty years of peace.

      The narrator says he “ruled it well for fifty winters.” Dying, Beowulf says it himself: no neighbouring king dared to face him. He used his strength and reputation to keep the kingdom at peace.

    5. He dies for his people.

      He goes to the dragon “as king of the people” and dies from the fight. His selflessness and sacrifice for his people is where he changes most.

    6. Proving himself.

      On his first night in Denmark he gives up his weapons and boasts that he is as dangerous as Grendel. Both moments are about proving his strength and building his reputation.

    Why a braid and not a timeline.

    A normal timeline puts Grendel at the start and the dragon at the end, with fifty years in between, so it just looks like Beowulf grew up and got old. Using John Leyerle's idea of interlaced storytelling, our braid shows Beowulf's roles as a warrior and a leader developing at the same time, instead of one after the other.

    Text evidence

    The four passages, in full.

    Beowulf changes from a confident, proud warrior into a responsible king. When he fights Grendel, he seeks to prove his strength and glory by fighting without any weapons. After ruling for fifty years, he becomes more wise. He fights the dragon because he feels it is his duty to protect his people. His courage and self-pride remain, since he does not want to line up a huge army before the fight, but his leadership shifts from personal fame to sacrifice and defending his people.

    Text evidence 01

    Fighting Grendel without weapons.

    I have heard moreover that the monster scorns
    in his reckless way to use weapons;
    therefore, to heighten Hygelac's fame
    and gladden his heart, I hereby renounce
    sword and the shelter of the broad shield,
    the heavy war-board: hand to hand
    is how it will be, a life-and-death
    fight with the fiend.

    Heaney, lines 433 to 440

    Beowulf demonstrates courage and fearlessness as he goes to fight Grendel.

    He has just arrived in Denmark and is telling King Hrothgar why he should be the one to face the monster. He gives up his weapons “to heighten Hygelac's fame,” so he is out to prove his strength and win glory, for his king and for himself.

    This is where our thesis starts: a young warrior using his strength for glory. Fifty years later he will use the same strength for his people.

    Text evidence 02

    Beowulf's confidence and desire for glory.

    And before he bedded down, Beowulf,
    that prince of goodness, proudly asserted:
    “When it comes to fighting, I count myself
    as dangerous any day as Grendel.
    So it won't be a cutting edge I'll wield
    to mow him down, easily as I might.”

    Heaney, lines 675 to 680

    Beowulf demonstrates self-pride and his reputation.

    On the night Grendel attacks, he boasts in front of the warriors in Heorot. He calls himself “as dangerous any day as Grendel,” which puts him on the same level as the monster, and “easily as I might” shows he knows a sword would be the smart choice.

    The narrator calls him “that prince of goodness” in the same sentence that says he “proudly asserted,” so the poem praises him and points out his pride at the same time.

    Text evidence 03

    Beowulf as an older peaceful king.

    the wide kingdom
    reverted to Beowulf. He ruled it well
    for fifty winters, grew old and wise
    as warden of the land

    Heaney, lines 2207 to 2210

    Beowulf demonstrates his growth as a leader and a ruler for fifty years.

    After Hygelac and then his son Heardred are killed, the kingdom of the Geats passes to Beowulf. “Warden of the land” is a word for a guardian, not a fighter, and after ruling that long he has grown “old and wise.”

    Next to these lines we put the great gold buckle from the Sutton Hoo ship burial, the grave of a real Anglo-Saxon king. Its thirteen ribbon snakes are woven together without a break, the way fifty years of political stability hold a kingdom together.

    Text evidence 04

    His responsibility as king before fighting the dragon.

    Beowulf spoke, made a formal boast
    for the last time: “I risked my life
    often when I was young. Now I am old,
    but as king of the people I shall pursue this fight
    for the glory of winning, if the evil one will only
    abandon his earth-fort and face me in the open.”

    Heaney, lines 2510 to 2515

    Beowulf demonstrates his logical thinking of fighting for the protection of his people.

    The dragon has burned the Geats' homes. Beowulf fights it because he feels it is his duty to protect them, and “king of the people” comes before “the glory of winning.” His courage and self-pride remain, though: he does not want to line up a huge army.

    The purse lid from the same royal grave shows a man standing alone between two beasts, which is how Beowulf goes to the dragon.

    Critical source

    Leyerle says a hero cannot also be a good king.

    John Leyerle, “The Interlace Structure of Beowulf” (1967)

    Leyerle, in his article “The Interlace Structure of Beowulf,” addresses many parts of Beowulf's character, including his heroic representation and how it clashed with his role as king. Leyerle acknowledges what it meant to be a hero in Anglo-Saxon culture and what it meant to be a king:

    “The fatal contradiction at the heart of heroic society was the tension between the heroic code, which demanded single-minded personal courage and individual prowess in battle, and the needs of a society that required prudence, foresight, and political wisdom in its leaders.”

    Leyerle, page 8

    However, despite Leyerle's claim that an Anglo-Saxon hero and a gracious ruler cannot be one and the same, Beowulf's evolution as a leader disputes this. Early in the poem, Beowulf displays a great ego. While Leyerle claims he never learns sacrifice, he mislabels Beowulf's ultimate sacrifice as selfishness:

    “By seeking personal glory in solitary combat, Beowulf wins the dragon's hoard but leaves his people leaderless and exposed to total destruction.”

    Leyerle, page 14

    Beowulf died for his people after they abandoned him at the sight of a dragon. In doing so, Beowulf represents both the Anglo-Saxon image of a hero and of a leader, having the wisdom to bring an army to defend the kingdom and the courage to fight alone after abandonment, both of which display the raw individual strength of a hero and the political wisdom required of a leader.

    Our positionBeowulf's growth as a leader disputes Leyerle: he is both the hero and the leader.

    Counter-reading

    Some say Beowulf never changes.

    The counter-reading

    Some may think Beowulf's leadership never changes throughout the poem, claiming he is a constant warrior brute who never puts his people's lives and interests first.

    “This fight is not yours, nor is it up to any man except me to measure his strength against the monster or to prove his worth. I shall win the gold by my courage…”

    Heaney, lines 2532 to 2536

    This points to his selfish desire for gold and personal victory, and since it is the last fight of his life, he never gets to repent or reflect on his selfish actions.

    Our response

    When put in context, this quote loses its force. Beowulf is speaking directly to the small army he brought with him. He reveres the lives of his followers too much to risk them, and he reassures them with his statement of confidence, the belief and skill a warrior commander on the battlefield should lead with.

    Another counter-reading

    Another counterargument is that Beowulf's leadership is stagnant: despite his people's trust, he never grows beyond an egotistical individual fighter who leads through strength rather than wisdom.

    “For fifty years I ruled this nation. No king of any neighbouring clan would dare face me with troops, none had the power to intimidate me.”

    Heaney, lines 2732 to 2736

    Our response

    While Beowulf always was a strong warrior, he uses his strength to become a strong king as well. This quote disproves that Beowulf is only a warrior brute, showing he uses his reputation to keep the kingdom at peace.

    Curatorial statement

    Why this exhibit.

    Throughout the text, Beowulf is portrayed as a strong character and a leader among the Geats; however, despite his constant presence and praise as a leader, the way he rules his people changes over time. At the beginning of the poem, Beowulf rules through sheer power and bloodline, using his ego to keep others in check, as he does with Unferth.

    Throughout the poem, Beowulf never loses his strength or reputation, yet despite no physical difference, he still matures and becomes a leader respected for his wisdom and cunning. This sentiment is addressed in Hrothgar's thanks to Beowulf: “I have never heard so young a man make truer observations. You are strong in body and mature in mind” (1842 to 1844). Hrothgar's thanks forebode Beowulf's next fifty years as a peaceful, strong ruler whose wisdom only grows.

    A direct comparison of Beowulf's wisdom in applying his strength appears in the fights against Grendel and the dragon, especially in how he prepares for each. Beowulf changes most throughout the poem in his selflessness and sacrifice for his people, culminating in his final fight and death against the dragon. Beowulf addresses his change in ego in a formal boast as he and his army prepare to face the dragon: “I risked my life often when I was young. Now I am old, but as king of the people I shall pursue this fight” (2511 to 2513).

    Beowulf represents an intriguing model of leadership throughout history. Throughout his life, Beowulf has earned the trust and respect of the Geats as a leader; however, despite having no criticism to change his arrogant warrior ways, he does, becoming in the process a greater leader for his people, eventually sacrificing himself for them.
