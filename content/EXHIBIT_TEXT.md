## Title, question, thesis (shown on the opening screen)

**Title:** Interlaced Beowulf (on the site as *Interlaced* / Beowulf)

**Driving question (Elam to approve):**
How does Beowulf change as a leader, and how does the way the poem is put together show it?
[OUR WORDS: our "Main question," plus the structure part the rubric asks for. Shortened for the opening screen; the longer version was "How does Beowulf's role as a leader change throughout the text, and how does the way the poem is put together shape how we see that change?"]

**Thesis (Elam to approve):**
We argue that Beowulf changes from a confident, proud warrior into a responsible king, but he never completely gives up the warrior code. Because the poem keeps connecting fights from different parts of his life, we can see that his courage stays the same while the consequences of his choices become more serious. His greatest strength, his courage, also becomes one of his greatest weaknesses.
[OUR WORDS: Liam's summary paragraph, the "Main Argument," and the last line of the counter-reading]

## The six passages

### Passage 1: Renouncing weapons (Warrior strand, ANTICIPATION)
**Lines 433 to 440** [FROM OUR DOC]
> I have heard moreover that the monster scorns
> in his reckless way to use weapons;
> therefore, to heighten Hygelac's fame
> and gladden his heart, I hereby renounce
> sword and the shelter of the broad shield,
> the heavy war-board: hand to hand
> is how it will be, a life-and-death
> fight with the fiend.

Note: check whether the book hyphenates "hand-to-hand."

### Passage 2: The boast in Heorot (Warrior strand, REPETITION)
**Lines 675 to 680** [FROM OUR DOC] [CHECK IN BOOK]
> And before he bedded down, Beowulf,
> that prince of goodness, proudly asserted:
> "When it comes to fighting, I count myself
> as dangerous any day as Grendel.
> So it won't be a cutting edge I'll wield
> to mow him down, easily as I might."

Fix applied: our doc had "So it won't be cutting edge." Heaney's line almost certainly has "a cutting edge." Confirm in the book.

### Passage 3: Hrothgar's warning (Leader strand wakes up, WARNING)
**Lines 1758 to 1762** [CHECKED: "eternal rewards"] [CHECK IN BOOK: line breaks]
> O flower of warriors, beware of that trap.
> Choose, dear Beowulf, the better part,
> eternal rewards. Do not give way to pride.
> For a brief while your strength is in bloom
> but it fades quickly

Fix applied: the old version said "eternal gains for your soul" and "sickness or sword's blow to cut you down." Neither is Heaney's wording. This passage was not in our doc; Gemini typed it from memory.

### Passage 4: Fifty winters (Leader strand, DEVELOPMENT)
**Lines 2207 to 2210** [FROM OUR DOC]
> the wide kingdom
> reverted to Beowulf. He ruled it well
> for fifty winters, grew old and wise
> as warden of the land

### Passage 5: The last boast (strands collide, COMPLICATION)
**Lines 2510 to 2515** [FROM OUR DOC]
> Beowulf spoke, made a formal boast
> for the last time: "I risked my life
> often when I was young. Now I am old,
> but as king of the people I shall pursue this fight
> for the glory of winning, if the evil one will only
> abandon his earth-fort and face me in the open."

Fix applied: our doc had the typo "make a formal boast."

### Passage 6: The cost (Leader strand, CONSEQUENCE)
Two short pieces from the end of the poem.

**Lines 2845 to 2847** [CHECK IN BOOK: exact lines]
> the battle-dodgers abandoned the wood,
> the ones who had let down their lord earlier,
> the tail-turners, ten of them together.

**Lines 3150 to 3155** [CHECK IN BOOK]
> A Geat woman too sang out in grief;
> with hair bound up, she unburdened herself
> of her worst fears, a wild litany
> of nightmare and lament: her nation invaded,
> enemies on the rampage, bodies in piles,
> slavery and abasement.

Fix applied: the old version quoted "Too few defenders rallied around the king" as lines 2845 to 2851. Those lines are about the ten deserters, not that sentence. The old 3150 to 3155 quote also dropped "enemies on the rampage" and changed "abasement" to "abjection."

## Counter-reading evidence (Blake)

**Lines 506 to 510** (Unferth taunting Beowulf about Breca) [CHECK IN BOOK]
> Are you the Beowulf who took on Breca
> in a swimming match on the open sea,
> risking the water just to prove that you could win?
> It was sheer vanity made you venture out
> on the main deep.

Note for Blake: this is Unferth talking, and he is hostile and drunk. That makes it stronger as a counter-reading if we admit it up front and then point out that Beowulf never denies the swim.

**Lines 2345 to 2347** (the poet, before the dragon fight) [CHECK IN BOOK]
> Yet the prince of the rings was too proud
> to line up with a large army
> against the sky-plague.

Blake's notes also list lines 2510 to 2537 and 3000 to 3120.
